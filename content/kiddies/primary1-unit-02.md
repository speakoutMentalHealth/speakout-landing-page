# Primary 1 Unit 2: Stories, Sounds and Numbers to Ten

## About this draft

Original SpeakOut, AI-assisted introductory material: three English and three Mathematics lessons following Unit 1. The sequence is a suggested delivery plan, not a national timetable or a locked progression. Primary 1 placement is provisional; select activities for the learner's current understanding. English Studies and Mathematics headings are confirmed in the national framework, but detailed revised Primary 1 outcome alignment and independent teacher validation remain pending. This small unit is not a full subject programme or a certified textbook.

## Facilitator preparation and pacing

Prepare paper, large drawings and a familiar book. Use drawn circles instead of small counters. Supervise pencil use and avoid coins, beads, sharp loose items, food, water play and recordings. Suggested sessions are about fifteen minutes with pauses. A child can direct an adult's drawing and does not need a login or a screen during the paper lesson.

Revisit Unit 1's factual story questions, sentences, small sound set and counting within five when needed. Numbers six to ten are an optional progression for a learner already comfortable with smaller visible groups, not a nationally verified threshold. Use home-language explanations to clarify meaning; model English when practising its vocabulary or sounds. Accept signing, pointing, oral answers, dictation or an established communication aid. Reading, handwriting and English fluency should not be mistaken for number understanding. Do not require speed, eye contact or personal disclosure.

## English Lesson 1: Put the story in order

Learner aim: identify the order of three stated events in a short original story. Read: “Bala picks up a book. He opens the book. Zainab looks at a picture with him.” Read aloud first; the learner does not need to decode the whole story independently.

Model: re-read one sentence at a time and sketch its action. Say, “First Bala picks up the book. Next he opens it. Then Zainab looks at a picture with him.” In this story, he refers to Bala. Explain that the ordering words help us talk about the sequence; they do not add new events.

Together: present the three drawings and ask what Bala does first. Re-read the first sentence if needed. Then ask what comes next and which event is last. The learner can direct the adult to put the drawings in order rather than physically moving anything.

Try: ask the learner to give a short retelling using first, next and then, or to choose the matching drawings while the adult narrates. Next, ask whether the story tells us the book's colour. Distinguish a stated fact from a detail the reader might imagine. A learner may need two events instead of three; simplify before increasing the language demand.

Check and answers: picks up the book first, opens it next, looks at a picture with Zainab last. The book's colour is not stated. A retelling with the same order and meaning is acceptable; exact wording is unnecessary. Next step: retell another short familiar sequence with the same support, rather than assuming one correct set of choices proves independent storytelling.

## English Lesson 2: Add one helpful description

Learner aim: build a meaningful sentence that includes one descriptive word. Prepare a drawing of a large book and a smaller book, with a table. Explain large and small by comparing the two books rather than treating either as an absolute measurement.

Model: read “The book is on the table.” Then read “The big book is on the table.” Say that big tells the reader more about which book we mean. Show the larger book. Point out the capital at the start and full stop at the end, keeping the message as the main focus.

Together: offer the words “The / small / book / is / on / the / table.” Read the words aloud before asking the learner to build the sentence. They can speak, dictate, point to the order or direct the adult's writing. Do not require cutting word cards or measure handwriting speed.

Try: use a new drawing where a large book is under a table. Invite a sentence that matches the picture. Ask which word describes the book's size and which word tells its position. The learner can choose between big/small or on/under if an open sentence is too demanding. Model a clear sentence without requiring repeated public correction.

Check and answers: “The small book is on the table.” is the expected word-order answer. A suitable sentence for the new drawing is “The big book is under the table.” Big describes size; under gives position. Several original sentences can be correct if they match the picture and intended meaning. Next step: choose one new familiar description after this structure is comfortable; do not treat the task as a complete lesson on all describing words.

## English Lesson 3: Meet the sound in mat

Learner aim: extend the introductory sound set with m and blend mat, while revisiting sat. This is a SpeakOut example sequence rather than a complete phonics programme. The teacher should check pronunciation, prior teaching and suitability before classroom use. Do not imply that every letter always represents one sound.

Model: show m and model the sound heard at the start of mat. Sustain that consonant gently without adding an extra vowel. Revisit the short a in sat and the starting sound of t in tap. Distinguish letter names from the sounds being blended. Written sound prompts need adult modelling; the website does not supply a pronunciation recording.

Together: write mat in large letters, indicate m-a-t and blend them into mat. Explain the word with a drawing of a floor mat. No child needs to touch or sit on a mat; the example is about meaning, not movement. Then blend s-a-t again and compare the beginning sounds.

Try: ask the learner to choose mat after hearing a modelled blend, then sat. If comfortable, invite a spoken blend or a matching word selection with their communication aid. Never infer independent reading from a repeated spoken model or a memorised button position. Present a fresh word order to check recognition.

Check and answers: m-a-t forms mat; s-a-t forms sat. M is the letter associated with the starting sound in mat in this example. If blending remains difficult, return to a single familiar sound or the original Unit 1 activities. Next step: practise the small set with a teacher's checked models; do not introduce many letters at once or use these tasks as a reading diagnosis.

## Mathematics Lesson 1: Build groups from six to ten

Learner aim: count visible groups of six to ten using one-to-one tracking and a final quantity statement. Begin with smaller groups from Unit 1 if needed. Reaching ten here is a proposed preparatory progression, not a national attainment claim.

Model: draw five circles in a row and one underneath. Track every circle once and count six in total. The second row does not start counting again at one when asking for the whole group's number. Then draw five circles and two more to show seven.

Together: prepare groups of eight, nine and ten as a row of five with the remaining circles underneath. Count each complete group slowly. An adult can indicate while the learner says or signs the words, or reverse those roles. Keep all circles visible; a grouping is a way to organise pictures, not an obligation to calculate mentally.

Try: ask for the number in a new group of seven and a new group of ten. Let the learner direct an adult to draw eight circles if appropriate. Tracking and the final quantity matter more than drawing neat circles. Reduce the group size when the task exceeds comfortable support.

Check and answers: five with one more is six; five with two more is seven; five with three more is eight; five with four more is nine; five with five more is ten. Reordering a group without adding or removing circles leaves its total unchanged. Next step: practise an already comfortable quantity in a different arrangement before increasing the range.

## Mathematics Lesson 2: Compare and order small groups

Learner aim: compare visible quantities within ten and put three familiar groups into order from fewer to more. Prepare groups with six, eight and ten circles. Use the same circle size and an accessible layout, so size of the drawing does not replace counting.

Model: compare six with eight by counting or pairing circles. Six has fewer; eight has more. Say that a widely spread group does not automatically contain more circles. The comparison refers to quantity rather than who responds faster.

Together: count the three prepared groups and label them six, eight and ten. Point from the smallest quantity to the largest and say “six, eight, ten”. A learner can indicate the order while the adult rearranges the labels or drawings. Inequality symbols are not needed for this check.

Try: show Group A with seven circles and Group B with nine and ask which has more. Then show two groups of eight with different spacing and ask whether the numbers are the same. Return to smaller quantities or pair-matching if tracking a larger set creates difficulty.

Check and answers: nine is more than seven; seven is fewer than nine. Two groups of eight contain the same number regardless of spacing. The prepared order from fewer to more is six, eight, ten. Next step: compare another familiar pair with visible support. Do not interpret choosing the larger printed numeral as understanding quantities without a suitable picture check.

## Mathematics Lesson 3: Join and take away within ten

Learner aim: show a joining or taking-away action using pictures, with a result within ten. Preserve all original circles so the learner can see what changed. Extend from Unit 1 only when the smaller examples are comfortable.

Model joining: draw five circles, then draw two more in another part of the same box. Count the whole set and record “5 + 2 = 7”. Explain which group was added. If needed, count all circles rather than insisting on counting on from five.

Together: draw six circles and add two more; count all eight and record “6 + 2 = 8”. On a new picture, draw eight circles and cross out two. Count the six not crossed out and record “8 − 2 = 6”. Crossed-out circles stay on paper as a record of the change but are not included in the remaining group.

Try: draw seven circles with two crossed out, and ask how many remain. Then draw four circles joined by three more and ask for the total. The learner can direct the drawing, count by pointing, choose a matching result or use an established aid. Do not require mental arithmetic or timed responses.

Check and answers: 7 − 2 = 5 and 4 + 3 = 7. For the earlier models, 5 + 2 = 7, 6 + 2 = 8 and 8 − 2 = 6. Ask the learner to indicate the joined or removed circles as well as the result. If they count crossed-out circles, revisit the remaining group. Next step: use another clear drawing within a comfortable range and record the support provided.

## English practice sheet

Adult directions: read the story and prompts aloud, allowing signing, pointing, dictation or a familiar aid. Prepare three simple story drawings and two different-sized books. Do not use the worksheet as an independent reading test unless that is already suitable for the learner.

Story: “Musa takes a book. He opens it. Amina looks at a picture with him.” Task E1: which event happens first? Task E2: what happens next? Task E3: who looks at a picture with Musa? Task E4: does the story tell us the book's colour? Task E5: order these words: “book / The / small / table / on / is / the”. Task E6: with an adult's checked sound model, blend m-a-t and s-a-t.

Answer guidance: E1 Musa takes a book; E2 he opens it; E3 Amina; E4 no, the colour is not stated; E5 “The small book is on the table.” E6 mat and sat. Accept retellings with the same meaning and order. Record the support used; modelled blending does not establish independent reading.

## Mathematics practice sheet

Adult directions: draw the actual groups before presenting the worksheet. Keep circles large and separated. Use one task at a time and revisit smaller quantities if appropriate. The learner can indicate a result or direct the adult's drawing without handwriting.

Task M1: draw seven circles and ask how many there are. Task M2: draw ten circles, arranged as five and five, and ask for the total. Task M3: draw seven circles in Group A and nine in Group B; ask which has more. Task M4: show two differently spaced groups of eight; ask whether they have the same number. Task M5: show five circles joined by two more. Task M6: show eight circles with two crossed out.

Answer guidance: M1 seven; M2 ten; M3 B; M4 yes, eight each; M5 seven, recorded as 5 + 2 = 7; M6 six, recorded as 8 − 2 = 6. Check the adult's drawings against these answers before the lesson. Changing the pictures without updating the answer notes invalidates the check. Different arrangements with the same quantity are acceptable.

## Learning check and next teaching step

Observe story ordering, descriptive sentences, the small sound set, one-to-one counting, comparison and the represented joining/taking-away action separately. The learner may understand number work while needing more English or communication support. A correct button choice after an explanation celebrates exploring; it does not prove independent mastery of the whole aim.

Use private planning descriptions such as demonstrated with current support, developing with modelling, or not observed yet. Record the example, response method, adult support and next small teaching step using a local code if needed. Do not collect personal disclosures, photos, voice recordings or home addresses in worksheets or the learning page. Fatigue and unfamiliar language are reasons to adapt, not grounds for a diagnosis or penalty.
