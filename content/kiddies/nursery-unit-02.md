# Nursery Unit 2: Patterns and Little Stories

## About this draft

Original SpeakOut, AI-assisted introductory material for supervised early learning. Unit 1 is a suggested starting point, not a prerequisite or an access restriction. Nursery class placement is adaptable. These four activities develop describing, ordering a short story, counting small visible groups and noticing a repeating pattern. They are SpeakOut learning aims, not a complete national programme. Independent teacher validation and exact outcome mapping remain pending. The NERDC one-year pre-primary reference is a broad source; it does not establish three Nursery age bands.

## Facilitator preparation

Prepare a familiar book, paper and large drawings of circles and squares. Use a thick pencil yourself to prepare pictures. Avoid small counters, beads, coins, loose batteries, sharp objects, food and water play. Supervise throughout. A child does not need an account, handwriting, a device or an internet connection during paper activities. Short sessions of around ten minutes are a suggestion, with pauses whenever wanted.

Revisit Unit 1 if the learner needs more support with familiar words or counting one to three. Accept pointing, signing, speaking, dictation or an established communication aid. Use home-language explanation to establish meaning before modelling English. Do not require touch, eye contact, rapid answers or personal disclosures. Present one task at a time; describing and number understanding should not be judged by English fluency or drawing skill. The interactive choices practise recognition; they do not prove independent storytelling or pattern construction.

## Lesson 1: A word that tells us more

Aim: communicate one useful description of a familiar object. Draw a small book and a much larger book side by side. Make both clearly recognisable and avoid details that distract from size.

Model: say, “These are both books. This book is big; this book is small.” Point to the relevant picture as you speak. Big and small are comparisons between these two pictures, not permanent labels for every book. If size is difficult to judge, enlarge the contrast or describe it aloud rather than repeating a test.

Together: invite the learner to indicate the big book, then the small book. Say a short phrase such as “a big book” and let the learner respond through their preferred method. An adult may supply the words while the learner selects a picture; record that support rather than treating it as an independently spoken phrase.

Try: reverse the pictures' positions and ask for the small book again. Then offer two new drawn circles of very different sizes. Ask whether the learner can use the same idea to choose the big circle. Do not use colour as the only distinguishing clue.

Check and answers: the bigger book is the big book; the smaller book is the small book, regardless of its position. The larger circle is the big circle. A description in the home language, a sign or a clear picture choice is acceptable. Next step: repeat a useful description with familiar objects, or revisit simple naming if comparing adds too much demand.

## Lesson 2: First and next in our story

Aim: recognise the order of two events in a short original story. Read: “Amina opens a book. Next, Amina looks at a picture.” An adult may sketch a closed book opening and a person looking at a page. Artistic skill is unnecessary.

Model: say, “First, Amina opens the book. Next, she looks at a picture.” Show the pictures in that order. Explain first and next by repeating the actions with a safe book; do not ask the learner to infer an unfamiliar sequence from pictures alone.

Together: read again and ask which event comes first. Offer the two drawings as choices. Then ask what happens next. Re-read the relevant sentence if needed. Pointing to a picture or directing the adult's arrangement can show the learner's thinking without a spoken sentence.

Try: use a new two-event story: “Musa sits beside the table. Next, Musa opens a book.” Ask for the first event and then the next. The learner need not act out sitting or move their body; the story can stay on paper. Do not require a particular posture or physical imitation.

Check and answers: Amina opens the book first and looks at the picture next. Musa sits beside the table first and opens a book next. Neither story tells us the colour of the book. A guessed detail is not a story fact. Next step: repeat two events with support; add another event only when the current task is comfortable, as a later extension rather than a requirement here.

## Lesson 3: Count up to five, one at a time

Aim: extend one-to-one counting towards four and five when groups of one to three are comfortable. This is a preparatory SpeakOut sequence; counting five is not asserted to satisfy a national outcome or Nursery age-band expectation.

Model: draw four circles in a clear row. Indicate one circle for each spoken counting word: one, two, three, four. Finish with “There are four circles.” Repeat with five circles on another page. Keep all pictures visible and clearly separated.

Together: let the learner indicate while the adult supplies number words, or let the adult track pictures while the learner speaks or signs. Agree an established response route for learners using eye-gaze or a communication aid. Do not treat an unestablished gaze or accidental touch as a reliable answer.

Try: draw a new group of four with different spacing and ask how many there are. Then show a group of five. The quantity comes from the pictures, not the size of the box. If a circle is skipped or counted twice, slow down and track it together. Return to one to three rather than insisting on the larger range.

Check and answers: a group drawn with four circles contains four; a group drawn with five contains five. Rearranging a group without adding or removing a circle does not change its number. Next step: practise tracking the same small quantity in another arrangement. Do not increase the range merely because the learner can recite number words.

## Lesson 4: What comes next in a pattern?

Aim: continue one simple repeating circle-square pattern. Prepare large drawn shapes in this order: circle, square, circle, square. Patterns are about a repeated order; sorting into groups is a different activity.

Model: point and say, “Circle, square; circle, square.” Pause after each pair. Explain that the same two-shape group repeats. Then draw a space after the last square and show a circle and a square as possible next shapes. Model choosing the circle because the pair begins again.

Together: practise another row beginning with a square: square, circle, square, circle. Ask what comes next, and let the learner indicate a choice. Say the row aloud and show the repeating pair again when helpful. Use shape and spoken names; colour recognition is not required.

Try: show circle, square, circle and ask for the next shape. An adult can draw the learner's selected shape. Do not require cutting, moving small pieces or precise handwriting. A learner may need to hear the sequence repeatedly or work with just one repeating pair.

Check and answers: after circle, square, circle, square comes a circle. After square, circle, square, circle comes a square. After circle, square, circle comes a square. Next step: repeat the same pair in a new row. Inventing a different pattern is an optional later extension; this unit practises recognising and continuing one rule.

## Reusable activity sheet

Adult preparation: make four clearly separated boxes on paper. Box A has a small book and a larger book. Box B shows Amina opening a book and then looking at a picture. Box C has four circles in one row and five in another. Box D has circle, square, circle, square followed by a space. Use large pictures and generous spacing. One box per session is enough if needed.

Prompt A: show the small book and describe or choose a description for it. Prompt B: choose what Amina does first, then next. Prompt C: count each row, tracking one circle per word or indication. Prompt D: choose a circle or square for the empty space and explain or indicate the repeating pair.

Answer guidance: A the smaller book; “small” is a useful description. B opens the book first, looks at a picture next. C four, then five. D a circle. Responses may be spoken, signed, pointed, dictated or supported through a familiar aid. Record adult support honestly. A matching picture choice is not proof of independent verbal explanation.

## Observation and next-session decision

Keep any planning notes private, using a local learner code if needed. Record the activity, response method, adult support and next small step. Useful descriptions include “selected the small book from two pictures after the words were repeated” or “counted four visible circles while the adult tracked them”. Never request photos, voice recordings, home addresses or personal mental-health disclosures for these activities.

Use demonstrated with current support, developing with modelling, or not observed yet as teaching notes rather than grades or developmental diagnoses. Fatigue, unfamiliar language or communication barriers can affect a response. Repeat an appropriate task with adjusted support before interpreting it. Stars on the website celebrate exploring, not mastery; the paper activity and the learner's actual response help the adult plan the next lesson.
