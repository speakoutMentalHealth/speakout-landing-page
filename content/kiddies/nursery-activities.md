# Nursery: Talk, Notice and Count

## 1. Welcome to shared learning

### For the adult
This activity book is an original SpeakOut draft for Nursery learning. It offers shared talking, listening, counting and pattern activities. It is not an official curriculum, a developmental test or a promise about what every child should achieve. A teacher should review the activities for the class, language, environment and individual learners before use. Older learners are welcome to explore them too. Nursery is the content label, not an access boundary.

Choose one small activity for a session. Read instructions yourself and invite the child to take part through speech, pointing, drawing, movement or another familiar form of communication. An adult operates the device; the child does not need an account, independent reading or continuous screen time to complete the activity. Print the page or copy the prompt onto paper if connectivity is weak. The suggested five-to-ten-minute activity window is flexible: pause sooner whenever the child wants a break.

Prepare a clear space and a few large, clean, familiar objects such as cups, folded cloths or empty cardboard boxes with smooth edges. Do not use coins, seeds, beads, bottle caps or other small loose pieces for these nursery activities. Check materials yourself, supervise throughout and remove damaged objects. Scissors, staples, sharp edges, cords and plastic bags are unnecessary. Paper drawings and the adult's hands can replace physical objects. There is no need to purchase a special learning kit.

Begin with an invitation: “Would you like to look at these pictures with me?” Offer two choices and accept “not now”. Sit where the child can comfortably see or reach. For a child who cannot comfortably handle the materials, let them direct you by pointing, looking, speaking or using their usual communication method. Ask the family or teacher about helpful adaptations; do not assume that one adaptation suits every child.

Use a language the child understands for discussion. You can model an English word alongside a familiar home-language word, but do not mark the home language as wrong. Allow a pause after each invitation. The adult's job is to respond to the child's idea, not demand a rehearsed answer. A quiet child can still participate through choosing, observing or gesturing.

### Try together
Place two large objects on the table. Say what you notice about one. Invite the child to choose the other and show one thing they notice. Finish by letting the child decide where the objects should go. Say, “You chose the cup. I saw you point to it.” This describes participation without comparing the child with classmates.

### Small learning record
Record the activity name, the date and one observation: “Pointed to a familiar object”, “Used a home-language word”, or “Preferred to watch today”. Keep this note with the responsible adult. Do not put children's full names, photographs, voices or personal stories into the platform for this activity. No public leaderboard or score is needed. Use the note to decide what to offer next, not to label the child.

## 2. Notice and name familiar things

### Learn together
Words are useful when they help us share something we can notice. Place a cup, a folded cloth and a book where the child can see them. Describe one item in a simple sentence: “This is a cup.” Then add one meaningful detail: “The cup is empty.” Invite a choice: “Which one would you like to talk about?” There is no required sequence of objects and no need to memorise a list of vocabulary.

If the child points without speaking, acknowledge the choice and model the word once. If the child uses a different name from their home language, accept it and ask the adult who knows that language to help with the English equivalent. Avoid pretending to know a translation. If the child says a longer sentence, respond to its meaning instead of interrupting every small language error. You can naturally repeat a clear version in your reply.

### The picture basket
Draw three simple pictures: a cup, a book and a cloth. They do not need to be realistic artwork. Place the real objects next to the pictures and invite the child to match one picture to an object. Then move the pictures and ask whether the match still works. The purpose is to notice a connection between a picture and what it represents, not to test drawing skill or speed.

Try a playful description: “I am thinking of something we can open and look inside. It has pages.” Let the child choose the book or offer another sensible idea. Ask, “What helped you choose?” A gesture towards the pages is an answer. If the description is confusing, show the object and explain. Do not turn an adult's unclear clue into a child's failure.

### Two useful words
Model two position words with the large objects: “beside” and “inside”. Put the cloth beside the empty box, then inside it. Ask the child to show one position or direct you to make it. Switch roles so the child can give a clue. Use pictures if handling objects is difficult. Avoid introducing many new position words in the same session.

### A tiny story
Tell this original story: “Amina took her book to the mat. Musa brought a cup. They put the cup beside the book. Amina opened the book and showed Musa a picture.” Ask who brought the cup and where they put it. The child may point to the cup, say a name or act out the placement. Retell the story together using your own objects and names if that makes it more familiar.

### Adult observation and extension
Notice whether the child identifies an object, connects a picture with it or uses a position word in any familiar language. Choose one observation, not a score for every prompt. Next time, swap in a different safe, familiar object. For a learner ready for more, invite two details such as size and use. For a learner who wants fewer choices, use only two objects and let them watch your first example.

## 3. Listen, respond and take turns

### A conversation game
A turn can be a word, a gesture, a drawing or a sound. Sit together with an empty cardboard box. Tap it once softly and invite a response. The child may tap, point, copy you or suggest another sound. Say what happened: “I made a sound, then you made one.” Keep the volume comfortable and stop immediately if a sound is unpleasant. Listening tasks should not require a child to tolerate noise or demonstrate hearing ability.

Move to a shared picture or familiar object. Say one sentence and leave room for the child to respond. You might say, “This cup could be in our pretend shop.” The child can decide what goes beside it. Build the conversation around that idea. You do not need to ask a question after every sentence. A comment can invite participation without turning the whole conversation into an examination.

### Copy and change
Make a simple two-part pattern using actions the child can comfortably do: a gentle hand tap followed by a wave. Show the pattern slowly and invite the child to copy or direct you. Then let the child invent a change. They might replace the wave with a point. Repeat their pattern so they see that their contribution matters. A seated gesture, eye movement or picture choice can replace standing or large movements.

Describe the order using “first” and “next”. Ask the child to show what comes first, not to repeat a long instruction. If following both actions is difficult, try one action and alternate turns. If the task is easy and enjoyable, introduce a third action only when the child wants to. A successful session is shared participation, not reaching the longest possible pattern.

### Our turn-taking story
Read this original story aloud: “Bisi and Tunde wanted to draw on the same sheet. Bisi drew a circle. Tunde added a line. Bisi made another mark. Tunde said, ‘Now my turn.’ They looked at their picture together.” Ask what each child added. Invite the learner to choose one mark for a shared picture. Add your own mark after theirs and name whose turn it is.

If the child wants to draw continuously, offer a second sheet rather than forcing them to give up their work. You can model a shared turn on another activity. Avoid using a learning task to require physical contact, obedience to every adult request or sharing of a personal item. The activity should be a manageable invitation, with the learner's choices respected.

### Words that help
Practise a few practical phrases through pretend play: “My turn, please”, “Help, please”, “Again”, and “I want to stop”. Accept equivalent words or gestures. Model listening when the child uses a stopping signal. End the session calmly; do not make continuing a condition for praise or access to ordinary care.

### Reflection for the adult
Which invitation produced a response? Was a gesture easier than a spoken answer? Did the child create a new pattern? Note one example privately. In a group, give every child space to contribute without ranking volume, speed or confidence. A teacher can revisit the same activity with quieter materials, fewer turns or a different communication method.

## 4. Count one object at a time

### What counting means here
In this activity, counting connects a number word with each object in a small group. Start with one, two or three large objects. The goal is not to recite a long number sequence. Put three large cups in a line. Touch or point towards one cup as you say “one”, another as you say “two”, and the last as you say “three”. Keep each cup visible and leave enough space to distinguish them.

Explain the last number simply: “There are three cups altogether.” Invite the child to move or direct you to move the cups into a different arrangement. Count again and notice that there are still three. Changing the arrangement does not add or remove a cup. If the child recounts from the beginning, allow it. Recounting is a useful way to check, not a mistake to discourage.

### Make a small set
Say, “Can we put two cups beside the box?” The child can choose the cups, point to them or tell you which to move. Count together afterwards. If there are three cups, describe what you see and invite a check: “We have three here. We wanted two. What could we move?” Demonstrate moving one away if needed. Avoid demanding the correct answer repeatedly after the child has lost interest.

Use drawings when objects are unavailable. Draw three large circles with clear spaces between them. Point to one circle for each count. Ask the child to choose a group of two by pointing while you cover the other circle with paper. Keep the activity free from small counters and loose pieces. There is no need to use food as a reward or counting material.

### An empty group
Show an empty space beside the cups. Ask, “How many cups are here?” Model “none” and “zero” if helpful. Zero describes that the group has no cups. Put one cup into that space and describe the change. Move it away again. A child can show the answer by pointing to the empty space; they do not have to recognise a written numeral.

### Match groups and number marks
An adult may write 1, 2 and 3 on separate sheets and put each beside a matching group. Explain that the written mark represents the number. Let the child choose a match or watch you. Distinguish recognising the symbol from counting objects: a learner may count accurately before they remember the written mark. Do not treat handwriting as a requirement for demonstrating quantity.

### Adult checks
Watch for touching or indicating each object once, starting with the first object and using the final count for the total. If an object is counted twice, separate the objects or move each counted cup to a new space. If an object is skipped, model a slower count. Record the support that helped. Repeat with a new arrangement on another day; do not turn a brief shared activity into a timed drill.

## 5. Sort, compare and continue patterns

### Make a rule together
Sorting means putting things together because they share a feature. Use large, familiar objects or your own paper drawings. You might draw two cups and two books. Put the cup pictures in one group and the book pictures in another. Say the rule aloud: “These show cups; these show books.” Invite the child to choose where another picture belongs and explain or indicate why.

A different reasonable rule can also work. A child may want to group pictures by colour instead of what they show. Ask them to demonstrate their rule, then follow it yourself. If an object could fit two groups, discuss that possibility. The activity is about noticing and explaining features, not guessing the adult's hidden answer.

### Compare without guessing from size
Put two cup pictures in one row and three cup pictures in another. Align one picture from the first row with one from the second. Notice that the second row has one extra picture. Say, “This group has more cups.” Now spread the row of two farther apart. Ask whether spacing added a cup. Count or match again to check. A row that looks longer does not always contain more objects.

Use “more”, “fewer” and “the same number” with small groups. Make two groups of two and match them in pairs. Explain that neither group has an extra object. If the comparison is confusing, keep to two groups and the same kind of picture. Do not mix object size, number and colour changes all at once.

### Continue a two-part pattern
Draw a cup, a book, a cup and a book in a line. Name the pattern slowly. Ask what could come next and let the child point to a choice. Add the cup picture, then ask what follows it. The repeated unit is cup–book. Move to another pattern only after discussing this one. A pattern can be made with shapes, gestures or spoken words, not only colours.

Let the child design a repeating pattern. They may choose circle–line, wave–tap or another comfortable pair. Ask them to show you the part that repeats. If they make a non-repeating design, describe it as their design and offer to build a repeating pattern together on a separate page. Creative drawing does not need to be corrected into a mathematics task.

### Find and repair a change
Make cup–book–cup–cup. Say, “I changed our pattern. Can you notice where?” Let the child move a picture, ask you to move it or explain what they would change. Then repeat the corrected pattern together. Present this as checking shared work, not catching a child's mistake.

### Adult observation
Record whether the learner sorted by a stated feature, matched two small groups or continued a repeating unit. Choose the activity they enjoyed most for the next session. A learner who finds colour difficult can use shapes or object types; a learner who does not use spoken language can place, point or direct an adult. Keep the rule visible and use larger, clearer pictures when needed.

## 6. Our pretend shop and next steps

### Bring the ideas together
Set up a pretend shop with drawings of cups, books and cloths. Use no money, food or small loose pieces. The shop is a shared language and number activity, not a lesson about real prices. Invite the child to be the customer or the shopkeeper. An adult can perform the handling while the child makes decisions through their usual communication method.

Start with one request: “I would like two cups, please.” The shopkeeper chooses a group and you count together. Place the cups beside a drawn book and describe the position. Swap roles if the child wants to. Make one deliberate, friendly error, such as offering one cup instead of two, and say, “Let's check our order.” Model correcting your own work calmly.

Next, ask the shopkeeper to sort the pictures into groups. They can choose the sorting rule. You might finish by arranging cup–book–cup–book along the counter and inviting the child to continue the pattern. Combine only familiar tasks. If the activity becomes confusing, return to a single request and one small group.

### A new original story
Read: “Sadiya opened a pretend shop on her mat. Her uncle asked for two books. Sadiya gave him one book, then counted again. She added another book. They put the two books beside an empty box. Her uncle asked how many books were inside the box. Sadiya pointed to the empty space and smiled.” Ask what Sadiya changed after checking. Ask how many books were inside the box. The story allows the child to show two and zero in meaningful situations.

Retell it using the learner's preferred names or language. Change “uncle” to another familiar caregiver if helpful. Family examples should not require every child to have the same household structure. Let the learner invent a different ending or ask you to read the story again. Enjoying a familiar story is a valid use of the session.

### A gentle review
Offer three separate invitations over time: choose and describe an object; make a group of two or three; continue a familiar two-part pattern. Provide the same supports used during practice. Do not surprise the child with a formal test or remove their communication supports to see whether they can perform independently. Record what they do and how you helped.

Use notes such as “Counted three pictures with pointing support” or “Created a cup–book pattern”. Avoid labels such as “behind”, “lazy” or “not clever”. A learning observation does not establish a diagnosis or a fixed ability. Discuss useful adjustments with the teacher or caregiver through an appropriate private channel.

### Plan another session
Choose one next activity based on the learner's interest. Repeat with different large objects, introduce a new position word, compare equal groups or retell a new two-person story. Move from three objects to four or five only when the learner is interested and the task remains comfortable. Finishing this activity book does not prove readiness for a particular class or guarantee entry to Primary.

The teacher review should check every task, language example, material choice, accessibility adaptation and class label. Confirm that adults can deliver it without special purchases or reliable internet. Mark revisions and trial observations before approving a student-facing edition. Keep the activity book free of identifiable child information and report concerns through the school's established safeguarding process, not through a public lesson comment.
