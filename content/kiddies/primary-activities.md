# Primary: Read, Explain and Solve

## 1. Getting ready to learn

### About this activity book
This original SpeakOut draft offers supplementary reading and number activities for review with Primary 1–3 teachers. Its provisional class labels describe the content, not who may access it. Learners from any education section may explore it. It is not an official Nigerian curriculum, a complete term's teaching or a certified assessment. Teachers should check the examples, local language needs, class placement and accessibility before approving a student-facing edition.

An adult should first read each activity and choose an appropriate starting point. The learner may read independently, listen to an adult, point, speak, draw or use their usual communication method. These different routes should not be treated as different levels of effort. Understanding a story is separate from handwriting, and solving a number problem is separate from operating a device.

Prepare paper, a pencil if comfortable, and drawings of familiar objects. The activities do not require purchases, coins, food, small counters or an internet connection during the lesson. Use large pictures that are easy to distinguish. An adult can print or copy a page before a session. If sharing one device with a group, the facilitator reads the prompt and learners work with their own paper or discuss responses in turns.

Choose a short session and leave time for a pause. The suggested ten-to-fifteen-minute session is a planning option, not a requirement. A learner may need more time, fewer tasks or a different communication format. Offer help without turning every hesitation into a test. Ask, “Would you like me to read it with you?” and accept the answer.

### Our learning habits
Begin with three practical habits: look or listen for useful information; try one step; check whether the answer fits the question. Model them yourself. Say, “I thought there were four pictures, but I counted again and found five.” This shows that checking is part of learning. Avoid comparing children's speed or rewarding only the first answer.

Use the home language for discussion where it helps the learner explain an idea. Model English vocabulary clearly when English is the subject of the activity, while recognising that mathematical understanding can be demonstrated in another language. Ask a knowledgeable adult about unfamiliar translations instead of guessing. A teacher may adjust names and familiar objects without changing the mathematical quantities or the meaning of a reading question.

### A first shared task
Write or read: “Ada has a book. She puts it on the table.” Ask what Ada has and where she puts it. The learner can answer with words, a picture or a pointing choice. Then draw three books and ask how many there are. Discuss how the reading question and the counting question need different information. Neither requires a long written response.

### Keep useful notes
A responsible adult can privately record the activity, date, support used and one example of the learner's reasoning. Do not collect photographs, voice recordings, full names or personal disclosures for this pack. A note such as “Explained the story after it was read aloud” is more useful than a fixed ability label. Let this record guide another activity, not a public ranking.

## 2. Read for meaning and tell it again

### Our original story
Read aloud or share this short story:

“Zainab and Chidi were arranging books in their classroom. Zainab placed the storybooks on a low shelf. Chidi found a book under a chair. Its cover was dusty, so he wiped the cover with a clean cloth. They put the book beside the other storybooks. Before leaving, they checked that the path to the door was clear. Their teacher thanked them for explaining where everything belonged.”

Read it once for enjoyment, then discuss it. The learner does not need to answer questions while hearing the story for the first time. If reading independently, invite them to pause at a full stop and check whether the sentence makes sense. Offer to read an unfamiliar word, then return to the sentence's meaning. Do not make sounding out every word the only route to participating.

### Find information in the text
Ask where Chidi found the book. The text says it was under a chair. Ask what he used to wipe the cover. The text names a clean cloth. Invite the learner to point to or repeat the relevant part of the story. Explain that a useful answer comes from the text, not from remembering what happened in a different classroom.

Now ask why the children checked the path to the door. The story does not give a spoken explanation, but it describes making the path clear. A reasonable answer is that people should be able to move through it without books in the way. Explain that this answer combines a detail in the story with an ordinary inference. Accept another sensible explanation grounded in the text; do not require one exact sentence.

### Beginning, middle and end
Draw three empty boxes. In the first, place or write the children arranging books. In the second, show Chidi finding and cleaning the book. In the third, show the books in place and the clear path. The learner can draw, dictate or arrange adult-written labels. The artwork does not need to be judged for neatness.

Ask the learner to retell the story using “first”, “next” and “finally”. They may use different words as long as the order makes sense. If an event is missing, return to the text together. Compare two retellings and notice that they can both be accurate even when they use different sentences.

### Another ending
Invite a new ending beginning, “The next morning…” Label it as the learner's invented continuation, not something stated in the original story. This distinction helps separate what a text says from what we imagine. Do not ask for a personal story about a difficult event; a fictional classroom gives enough material for the activity.

### Review together
Check three things: a factual answer matches the text; a retelling keeps the main events in order; an invented ending is identified as invented. For a learner ready for more, ask which sentence helped them answer. For a learner who needs fewer choices, use two picture options and read the relevant sentence again. Privately note the support that made understanding easier.

## 3. Build clear sentences and useful questions

### A sentence shares an idea
Start with “The book is on the shelf.” Read it aloud and point out that it tells us something complete about the book. Write the words on paper or simply say them together. Ask the learner to choose a different familiar object and place. You might make “The cup is on the table.” Keep the meaning clear before discussing spelling or handwriting.

Model a capital letter at the start and a full stop at the end. Explain that these marks help a reader see where the sentence begins and finishes. They are useful tools, not the whole purpose of writing. If a learner dictates a sentence for an adult to write, they can still decide the idea, word order and punctuation. Invite them to show the start and end in whatever way is comfortable.

### Repair a mixed-up message
Offer “shelf on is book the the” and ask what might make the message easier to understand. Rearrange it to “The book is on the shelf.” Read both versions and compare. A learner can move large word cards, point while an adult moves them or say the words in order. Use only a few words initially. Small cards are unnecessary; a whole sheet with large separated words works too.

Try two sentences that change one detail: “The book is under the table” and “The book is on the table.” Act them out with a drawing or safe object. Ask what changed. This shows that a small word can change meaning. Avoid introducing many grammar labels before learners understand what the sentence communicates.

### Ask to find out
Turn the statement into a question: “Where is the book?” Explain that this question asks for its place. Practise “Who found the book?” for a person and “What did Chidi use?” for an object. Read the classroom story again and match each question to information it requests. A useful question should be understandable and answerable from the available information.

Model a question mark at the end. Compare “The book is on the shelf.” with “Is the book on the shelf?” Notice that one tells and the other asks. The learner can respond by speaking, pointing or showing a choice. Do not require personal information such as a home address to practise asking questions.

### Make a small message
Invite the learner to create a fictional note for a classroom shelf: “Please put storybooks here.” Discuss who will read the note and what they need to know. They may add a simple picture. Read the message together and ask whether a reader could understand it without guessing. A short, clear message is useful; it does not need to become a long composition.

### Adult feedback
Respond first to meaning: “I know where you want the books to go.” Then select one teaching point, such as a full stop or a clearer place word. Do not mark every issue at once. Keep a first attempt and a revised attempt privately if useful. Describe the revision as improving the message, not fixing a learner who is “bad at English”. Teachers should adapt examples to the language and literacy work already being taught.

## 4. Join and separate small groups

### Start with a drawing
Draw four large circles in one group and three in another. Explain that the circles represent books, without claiming they are realistic pictures. Ask how many books there would be if the groups were joined. The learner may count each circle, count on from four or use another understandable method. There are seven altogether. Write 4 + 3 = 7 and read it as “four plus three equals seven”.

Connect each symbol to the drawing. The plus sign shows joining the groups in this example. The equals sign shows that both sides represent the same quantity. It does not mean “write any answer after this”. Draw seven circles on a second page and compare them with the joined groups. Ask what is the same even though the arrangement is different.

### Tell a number story
Read: “There are four books on a shelf. A helper adds three books. How many books are on the shelf now?” Ask what we know and what we need to find. The two known amounts are four and three. The unknown amount is the new total. Let the learner draw or explain before writing a number sentence.

Change only one quantity for a second problem: “There are four books. Two more are added.” The total is six. Ask what changed from the first story. Comparing the problems helps the learner notice the number information, instead of memorising that every story about books has the same answer.

### Take some away
Draw seven books. Cross out two and leave the other five visible. Explain that the crossing-out represents two books being moved elsewhere. Write 7 − 2 = 5. Ask how many remain, not how many marks are on the page altogether. The moved books have not disappeared from the world; they are no longer in the group being counted.

Read: “A shelf has seven books. Two are taken to a table. How many stay on the shelf?” Ask which group the question means. A learner who answers seven may be counting books in both places. Return to “stay on the shelf” and show the two locations. Treat this as interpreting the question, not simply making an arithmetic error.

### Check by reversing the action
If five books remain and two were moved, joining those groups again gives seven. Write 5 + 2 = 7 next to 7 − 2 = 5. Discuss how the two statements describe connected actions. Let the learner check by drawing or counting. They do not need to use a particular mental method to have a valid answer.

### Practice and explanations
Offer 3 + 2, 6 − 1 and 5 − 5 with pictures as needed. The answers are five, five and zero. For the last example, an empty group remains. Ask the learner to explain one answer using a sentence, a drawing or directed pointing. Avoid timed competition. A teacher can adjust the number range and notation to the class's current work; this draft uses small whole-number groups only.

## 5. Tens, ones and fair comparison

### Make a group of ten
Draw ten large marks together and enclose them in a clear boundary. Explain that this group can be counted as one ten. Draw three separate marks beside it. There are thirteen marks altogether: ten and three more. Write 13 and describe its two digits in this example: one ten and three ones. The digit 1 is not saying there is only one object in the whole drawing.

Make another drawing with one group of ten and five separate marks. That is fifteen. Compare thirteen and fifteen. Both have one ten, but fifteen has two more ones. Match the ones in pairs to check. Use large paper drawings or ten frames; small counters, money and purchased equipment are not necessary.

### Keep the amount when regrouping
Show thirteen separate marks, then draw a boundary around ten of them. Ask whether adding the boundary created more marks. It did not. The grouping helps us describe the amount more conveniently. Count all thirteen again if the learner wants to check. Explain that an amount can be represented in different ways without changing it.

Now show two groups of ten and no separate ones. There are twenty. Discuss the written number 20: two tens and zero additional ones. Zero has a useful role in the notation. Do not replace this explanation with a rule about ignoring zero; that would make the number confusing.

### Compare the groups that matter
Compare 12 and 21 through drawings. Twelve has one ten and two ones; twenty-one has two tens and one one. Twenty-one is greater even though twelve has more separate ones. Count the whole amounts or compare tens first. A teacher may use “greater than”, “less than” and “equal to” after learners can explain the quantities. Symbols can be introduced separately, not as a speed trick.

Try an equality comparison: ten and four ones is the same quantity as fourteen individual marks. The layouts differ but the amounts match. Ask the learner what evidence supports “the same”. Pointing to paired marks, counting or describing tens and ones are all useful explanations.

### A small school problem
Read: “One box has ten notebooks and four extra notebooks beside it. Another box has ten notebooks and two extra notebooks. Which collection has more?” Both collections contain one ten. Comparing four extra with two extra shows the first has two more. The problem is about collections, not the physical size or colour of the boxes.

### Adapt and extend
A learner new to this idea can stay with numbers eleven to fifteen and clear grouped drawings. A learner ready for more can represent twenty-three with two tens and three ones, then compare it with twenty-five. Do not infer a permanent ability from one response. Teachers should check that the range, vocabulary and place-value representations fit the class before approval. Record whether the learner counted all, grouped ten or compared groups, so the next session can build on a visible method.

## 6. A reading-and-number mini-project

### Read the planning note
Share this original note:

“On Friday, a class will arrange a small reading shelf. There are six storybooks on the table and four storybooks in a box. The class wants all ten books on the shelf. Three helpers will work together. One will check the books, one will arrange them and one will keep the path clear. Afterwards, they will write a short note to show where the storybooks belong.”

The project is fictional; it does not require learners to move furniture, handle cleaning chemicals or rearrange a real classroom. Use drawings. An adult is responsible for any real-world activity beyond the paper task. The learner can discuss roles without being assigned responsibility for another person's safety.

### Find and organise information
Ask what the class wants to arrange, how many helpers there are and what note they plan to write. The answers are a reading shelf, three helpers and a note showing where storybooks belong. Ask which sentence supports each answer. Then draw a table group of six books and a box group of four books. Label their locations so the quantities do not become detached from the story.

Join the two groups in a shelf drawing and write 6 + 4 = 10. Check the total by counting all ten pictures or explaining that four more after six reaches ten. A learner may demonstrate counting on slowly with an adult; fluent mental calculation is not the only acceptable method.

### Ask a new question
Say, “Two of the ten books will be read at a table. How many remain on the shelf?” Draw the move and solve 10 − 2 = 8. Check by joining the eight shelf books with the two table books. Explain that the total collection still contains ten books, while eight are in the location the question asks about.

Invite the learner to invent a number question using the same pictures. Make sure the question includes enough information to answer. If a quantity is missing, ask what information a reader would need. Label this as improving a question, not rejecting the learner's idea.

### Make the shelf note
Create a short message, such as “Storybooks go on this shelf.” Read it aloud and check that it tells a reader what to place and where. Add a capital letter and full stop. A learner may dictate the note, draw a supporting picture or write it. Compare two different clear messages and discuss why both work.

### Review without ranking
Ask the learner to show one text detail, one number method and one clear sentence. Keep familiar supports available. Record a specific observation, for example, “Used the drawing to explain why eight stayed on the shelf.” This is a practice record, not a certified score or evidence of completing Primary 1, 2 or 3. Do not publish children’s results or compare them on a leaderboard.

Before student publication, a teacher should review the arithmetic, reading questions, answer notes, language demand and class labels. Check that the original stories are locally understandable and that alternatives work for learners who do not write, speak or see the same way. Trial one activity with appropriate consent and record only de-identified teaching observations. Use findings to revise the pack. This draft provides supplementary practice, not a complete curriculum or a replacement for classroom teaching.
