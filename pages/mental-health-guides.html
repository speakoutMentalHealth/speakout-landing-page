Practical mental health guides for everyday life

Clear, supportive information for young people, parents, caregivers and educators. These guides support awareness and early help-seeking; they do not diagnose a condition or replace assessment and care from a qualified health professional.

SpeakOut Mental Health Outreach · Educational resource · Reviewed November 2026

1. Understanding anxiety

Anxiety is the body's response to a perceived threat or pressure. It can show up as repeated worry, restlessness, irritability, difficulty concentrating, a racing heartbeat, tense muscles or changes in sleep. Feeling anxious sometimes is a normal human experience; the intensity, duration and effect on daily life matter.

What may help in the moment

  • Pause and breathe gently, making the out-breath a little longer than the in-breath. Stop if you feel dizzy or uncomfortable.
  • Name what is happening: “I am noticing worry; I do not have to solve everything at once.”
  • Use grounding: identify five things you can see, four you can feel, three you can hear, two you can smell and one you can taste.
  • Break the next task into one small, realistic step. Reduce avoidable caffeine and protect regular sleep where possible.
  • Talk with someone safe and trustworthy rather than carrying the worry alone.

When to seek support

Speak with a qualified health professional if anxiety persists, becomes difficult to manage, disrupts school, work, relationships or sleep, or leads you to avoid everyday activities. Sudden or severe physical symptoms should not automatically be assumed to be anxiety; seek medical assessment when needed.

2. Managing exam stress

Exams can create pressure, especially when expectations feel high. Stress may affect sleep, appetite, concentration and confidence. A useful plan focuses on preparation and recovery rather than studying without breaks.

A practical study routine

  • Write down the topics and rank them by importance and confidence.
  • Set short study blocks with brief breaks. Choose one specific task for each block.
  • Use active recall: close the book and explain the idea, answer practice questions or teach the concept aloud.
  • Protect sleep, meals, hydration and movement. An all-night study session can make concentration and recall harder.
  • Ask a teacher, classmate or caregiver for help with topics you do not understand.

On the day

Arrive with the required materials, read instructions carefully and begin with a question you can attempt. If panic rises, pause briefly, breathe comfortably and return to one question at a time. An exam result is information about one performance; it is not a measure of your worth.

If stress becomes overwhelming or affects your safety, tell a trusted adult or qualified professional promptly.

3. How parents and caregivers can talk with teenagers

Young people are more likely to share when they feel listened to rather than judged or rushed. Choose a calm, reasonably private moment. Begin with an open invitation such as, “You seem to have a lot on your mind. Would you like to talk, or would you prefer that I just sit with you?”

Helpful ways to respond

  • Listen without interrupting. Reflect what you heard before offering advice.
  • Use curious, non-accusing questions: “What has been the hardest part?”
  • Acknowledge feelings without dismissing them: “That sounds difficult. Thank you for telling me.”
  • Ask what support would help and agree on a practical next step together.
  • Keep appropriate boundaries, but do not promise secrecy when someone may be at risk of harm.

If a young person may be unsafe

Stay calm, take the concern seriously and ask directly and sensitively whether they are thinking about harming themselves or someone else. Do not leave someone in immediate danger alone. Involve a qualified health professional and the appropriate safeguarding lead or emergency service. Share information only with people who need it to protect the young person.

4. What teachers can notice and do

Teachers are not expected to diagnose mental health conditions. They can notice changes, offer a respectful check-in and connect a learner with the school's designated safeguarding or wellbeing lead.

Changes worth checking in about

  • A sustained change in attendance, participation, academic performance or peer relationships.
  • Withdrawal, unusual agitation, frequent distress, or a marked change in behaviour.
  • A learner saying they feel unsafe, hopeless or unable to cope.

A safe first response

Speak privately where appropriate, describe the change you have noticed without labelling the learner, listen, and explain what will happen next. Follow school safeguarding procedures, document factual observations securely and refer concerns to the designated lead. Do not investigate abuse yourself, promise absolute confidentiality or share sensitive details in public staff channels.
