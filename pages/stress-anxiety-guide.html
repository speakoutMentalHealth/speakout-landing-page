Understanding stress and anxiety

Stress is a response to pressure or demands. Anxiety can involve worry, fear or a sense of threat, even when danger is not immediate. Both can affect sleep, concentration, relationships and daily activities. Having these feelings does not automatically mean someone has a mental health disorder.

What might you notice?

Common experiences include racing thoughts, irritability, headaches, tense muscles, stomach discomfort, restlessness, trouble sleeping or avoiding situations. Symptoms vary. A health professional can help assess symptoms that are persistent, intense or disruptive.

Five practical steps

  1. Name the feeling. Ask yourself what you are worried about and what is within your control.
  2. Slow your breathing. Breathe gently and comfortably; stop if you feel dizzy or unwell.
  3. Break tasks into smaller actions. Choose one achievable step rather than trying to solve everything at once.
  4. Protect basic routines. Make room for sleep, meals, movement and breaks where possible.
  5. Speak to someone safe. A trusted adult, friend, counsellor or qualified clinician can help you plan next steps.

For parents and teachers

Listen without ridicule or punishment. Ask open questions such as “What has been hardest lately?” Avoid promising absolute secrecy if someone is at risk. Help young people reach appropriate support and follow your school's safeguarding procedures.

When to seek professional support

Seek an assessment if worry or distress lasts, worsens, interferes with school, work, sleep or relationships, or causes repeated panic-like symptoms. Physical symptoms can have medical causes too, so do not assume anxiety is the explanation.

Safety first: If someone may harm themselves or another person, is in immediate danger, or has a medical emergency, contact local emergency services or go to the nearest emergency department. Stay with the person when safe to do so and involve a trusted responsible adult for a young person.

What this guide can and cannot do
