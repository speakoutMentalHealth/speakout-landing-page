Nursery Unit 1: Our Learning Space

About this draft

This is an original SpeakOut unit for adult-led early learning. It contains four short lessons and a reusable activity sheet. It is a teaching draft, not an official textbook, a complete term or a graded course. Nursery placement is provisional: select activities for the individual learner rather than assuming that Nursery 1, 2 or 3 means a nationally specified age band. The national pre-primary reference covers one year. A learner from another level may use these activities.

The unit brings together familiar-object vocabulary, taking conversational turns, counting small groups and sorting. These are SpeakOut learning aims. Vocabulary, turn-taking and sorting have candidate partial references in the NERDC pre-primary guide reviewed in 2024; the counting progression here has not been verified against its full expectations. Educational suitability, class placement, originality and rights review remain pending. Activities are AI-assisted original drafts; no teacher approval is claimed.

Facilitator preparation

Use a book, a large empty plastic cup, a cloth and paper. Check that objects are clean, intact and too large to swallow. Do not use coins, bottle caps, seeds, beads, sharp items or loose batteries. Keep drinking, food, water play and scissors out of this unit. An adult leads and supervises throughout. Activities can be spoken and copied onto paper; a child account or internet connection is unnecessary.

Plan about ten minutes per lesson, with a pause whenever needed. This is a suggested pace, not a requirement. Offer one object or prompt at a time. Accept pointing, signing, eye-gaze choices, spoken words or a familiar communication aid. Do not require eye contact, touch, public performance or a recording. Use the home language to establish meaning and model the English word gently if appropriate. Ask a knowledgeable speaker about unfamiliar translations rather than inventing them.

Lesson 1: Notice and name

Aim: the learner identifies a familiar object and associates it with a useful word or sign. Prepare the book, cup and cloth. Start with two objects if three choices are too demanding.

Model: hold the book and say, “This is a book. We can look at a book.” Allow time to explore visually or through a safe, optional touch. Hold the cup and say, “This is a cup.” Avoid a rapid vocabulary test. Name an object, pause, and let the learner choose how to respond.

Together: put the book and cup apart on a clear surface. Ask, “Can you show me the book?” If the learner chooses the cup, calmly name it: “You showed the cup. Here is the book.” Repeat with a smaller choice set instead of marking a failure. Then ask for the cup and introduce the cloth when the two-object task is comfortable.

Try: ask the learner to choose an object for the adult to name. This reverses the roles and creates a reason to communicate. Extend with one descriptive contrast, such as a soft cloth and a firm book cover. Do not imply that the learner must use the same words as the adult.

Check: show the objects in a different order and ask for one familiar object. Expected response: the requested object is indicated using any workable method. If a name is spoken in the home language, acknowledge it and provide the English model without treating the home-language response as wrong. Next step: repeat familiar words or add one new word, depending on the support needed.

Lesson 2: My turn, your turn

Aim: the learner participates in a short shared exchange. Use the book from Lesson 1 as a visual cue; it does not need to be passed physically.

Model: say, “My turn: I choose the book.” Then offer, “Your turn. What would you choose?” Wait quietly. Choosing, looking towards an option, signing, pointing or speaking can all be a turn. The adult can narrate a choice without putting words into the learner's mouth: “You pointed to the cup.”

Together: take three short turns, alternating adult and learner. The adult models waiting and listening. Avoid making stillness or silence the goal. A learner may move, hold a familiar calming object or need a break while still participating. Agree a simple pause signal; a gesture or communication aid is enough.

Try: read this original mini-story: “Amina opens a book. Musa looks at the picture. Amina asks, ‘Shall we look together?’ Musa chooses a place beside her.” Ask, “What did Amina open?” and offer the book and cup as choices. Then invite a turn to choose which object the characters could look at next. That next choice is an invented continuation, not a fact in the story.

Check: expected story answer is a book. Expected participation is one shared turn with an appropriate response method; no sentence is required. If participation stops, reduce the exchange to one adult turn and one learner turn. Do not insist that a learner continue after a pause request. Next step: use the same familiar exchange before increasing its length.

Lesson 3: One indication for each object

Aim: the learner practises counting a small group by indicating each item once. Prepare three large circles drawn well apart on paper. An adult can draw them beforehand. Use drawings rather than loose small counters.

Model: point to one circle and say “one”; point to the next and say “two”; point to the third and say “three”. Then say, “There are three circles.” Explain the method by doing it slowly, not through a long description. A counting word should go with one indicated circle.

Together: start with one circle and then two. Invite the learner to point while the adult supplies the words, or let the learner use words or signs while the adult points. For a learner using eye gaze, clearly present separate choices and agree an accessible way to indicate each item. Do not interpret an unestablished gaze response as a reliable answer.

Try: cover one of the three circles with a sheet and count the two visible circles. Uncover it and count all three again. The question is how many are visible now, not a formal subtraction test. Do not move objects quickly or require recall of a hidden set.

Check: with two circles visible, the answer is two; with all three visible, the answer is three. Look for one indication per visible circle and a final quantity response. If a circle is skipped or counted twice, model touching or tracking each circle once. Use the learner's preferred response method. Next step: repeat the small group in a different arrangement before making the group larger.

Lesson 4: Same feature, different groups

Aim: the learner groups pictures by one visible feature and can indicate the feature. Draw two large circles and two large squares on one sheet, clearly separated. Shapes need not be cut out or physically moved.

Model: point to the two circles and say, “These belong together because they are circles.” Point to the squares and say, “These are squares.” Trace the round outline and the four sides with your own finger if helpful, but do not require the learner to trace or touch.

Together: point to one circle and ask the learner to show another circle. Repeat with a square. Then invite the learner to indicate both members of one group. An adult can draw an outline around the chosen group. Grouping is about a feature, not which drawing is best or which child responds first.

Try: make a new sheet with one circle, one square, one circle and one square in a mixed arrangement. Ask, “Which pictures go together?” Accept a shape name, a gesture between matching pictures or a description such as “round”. If the task is difficult, show only one circle and one square and name each before rebuilding the group.

Check: the circles form one group and the squares another. “They have the same shape” is an adequate explanation; a formal geometry definition is unnecessary. Next step: repeat sorting by shape or, when secure, try a single new feature such as large versus small. Do not combine size and shape rules in the first check.

Reusable activity sheet

Adult preparation: copy four separate boxes onto a page. In Box A, draw a large book and cup, or place the safe real objects beside the page. In Box B, write “my turn / your turn” for the adult's cue. In Box C, draw three large circles separated by clear spaces. In Box D, draw two circles and two squares. Artistic quality is irrelevant; make choices visibly distinct.

Prompt A: “Show the book. Now choose something for me to name.” Answer guidance: the book is indicated for the first prompt; the second is an open choice. Prompt B: take one shared choice turn each. Guidance: a meaningful choice or pause signal counts as participation. Prompt C: cover one circle and ask how many are visible, then uncover it and ask again. Answers: two, then three. Prompt D: ask which pictures go together. Guidance: both circles together and both squares together, using shape as the feature.

Use only one box during an initial session if needed. The page is a reusable activity aid, not a scored worksheet. A learner may answer orally or through another established communication method; adult handwriting does not substitute for the learner's thinking.

Observation and next-session decision

Use a private note with a local learner code if a note is needed. Record the lesson, example of response, support offered and next teaching step. Useful wording is “identified the book from two choices after the word was repeated” or “counted two pictures once each while the adult supplied number words”. Do not record names, photos, voice recordings or personal disclosures in this public material or upload observations to the review page.

Use three planning descriptions: demonstrated with current support; developing with modelling; or not observed yet. These are not grades, a developmental diagnosis or a permanent judgement. A missed response can reflect communication demand, unfamiliar language, fatigue or the task presentation. Repeat a suitable task with adjusted support before making an instructional decision. Do not compare learners' speed or require every lesson in one sitting.

Primary 1 Unit 1: Words and Numbers Around Us

About this draft

This original SpeakOut starter unit combines three English lessons and three Mathematics lessons, with separate practice sheets and answer guidance. Primary 1 is a provisional teaching placement, not an access restriction. The unit is not a complete term, an official textbook, a certified course or an assessment for progression. It is an AI-assisted original teaching draft awaiting educational and rights review.

English Studies and Mathematics are verified subject headings in the official national framework. The specific learner aims and sequence below are proposed by SpeakOut: full revised Primary 1 outcomes have not been verified, so national outcome alignment is not claimed. Do not interpret a subject heading as proof that every lesson matches the current class curriculum. A teacher should check the sequence, language demand, phonics examples and mathematical representations before approving a learner edition.

Facilitator preparation and pacing

Prepare paper, a thick pencil if useful, and a book. Draw pictures rather than using small loose counters. No purchase, child login, internet connection during teaching or recording is required. Suggested sessions are about fifteen minutes, but split or extend them according to the learner. Teach English and Mathematics in separate short sessions if combining them would overload attention.

Use the home language to clarify a concept, while modelling English when practising English vocabulary. Accept oral, signed, dictated, pointing or established communication-aid responses. Mathematics understanding should not depend on English fluency or handwriting speed. Large print, clear spacing, fewer choices and reading prompts aloud are available adaptations. Do not demand eye contact, a timed response or a personal disclosure. Offer pauses without treating them as failure.

English Lesson 1: Listen for useful information

Learner aim: identify a character, an object and a place in a short original story. The learner can listen rather than read independently.

Model with this story: “Sade has a red book. She puts the book on a table. Tunde brings a pencil. They sit beside the table and look at the book together.” Read slowly once. On the second reading, pause after each sentence and let the learner notice one detail. Do not ask the learner to remember everything from a single hearing.

Together: ask “Who has the book?” Re-read the first sentence if needed. Then ask “What does Tunde bring?” and “Where does Sade put the book?” Model how an answer comes from a sentence: “I heard that Tunde brings a pencil, so my answer is a pencil.” Offer two picture or word choices if an open question creates excessive language demand.

Try: ask the learner to tell or indicate what happens first and what happens next. A suitable short retelling is that Sade has a book, puts it on the table, and Tunde brings a pencil before they look together. An adult can sketch these events as choices. Word-for-word recall is unnecessary.

Check and answers: Sade has the book; Tunde brings a pencil; the book is put on the table. The story does not tell us the colour of the pencil or whether either child owns the table. “The story does not say” is the appropriate response to those extra questions. This helps distinguish information in the story from a guess. Next step: repeat two factual questions with a new original mini-story, rather than increasing its length immediately.

English Lesson 2: Say, build and mark a sentence

Learner aim: communicate a simple complete idea and recognise its written start and end. Begin with meaning before punctuation or spelling.

Model: show or read “The book is on the table.” Ask what it tells us about the book. Then model a capital letter at the start and a full stop at the end. Explain that these marks help a reader find the sentence boundary. A learner who dictates can decide the idea and indicate the start/end while an adult writes.

Together: prepare the words “The / cup / is / on / the / table.” in large spaces on one page. Read them in order. Then deliberately read “table cup the on is the” and ask why the message is harder to understand. Let the learner point to or say a helpful order while an adult writes it. Cutting small word cards is unnecessary.

Try: offer “The book is under the table.” and “The book is on the table.” Use a simple drawing or the real book to illustrate each. Ask which word changes the position. Then invite an original sentence using book, cup or pencil. Accept a dictated sentence. Model grammar respectfully instead of requiring repeated correction of a hesitant speaker.

Check and answer guidance: “The cup is on the table.” is the expected arrangement. “On” and “under” change the position. The learner's own sentence has many possible valid answers, such as “The pencil is on the table.” Check that the intended message is understandable and the adult's written version matches it. If reviewing punctuation, identify the capital at the beginning and full stop at the end. Do not mark handwriting quality as sentence understanding.

English Lesson 3: Hear and blend a small sound set

Learner aim: explore how a few spoken sounds relate to letters and can be blended in a simple word. This is a proposed introduction, not a complete phonics programme. A teacher must confirm local pronunciation, prior teaching and the chosen progression.

Model the letters s, a and t, one at a time. Use the sound represented in “sat”: s as in the start of “sun”; the short a heard in “sat”; and t as in the start of “tap”. Avoid adding an extra “uh” after consonants. The letter name and its sound are different teaching targets; establish which one is being practised. Do not state that any letter always has only one sound.

Together: write “sat” in large letters. Point to s, a and t while modelling the three sounds, then say them closer together to form “sat”. Explain the meaning with “Sade sat beside the table.” Invite the learner to blend with you, listen and choose the matching word, or use an established communication aid. A spoken imitation is not required to demonstrate a choice.

Try: introduce p as in “pen” and blend t-a-p into “tap”. Explain “tap” as a gentle touch or knocking action, not water play. A learner may point to the whole word while the adult models. Do not use “the” or “table” as examples for this small sound set; those words need additional teaching and were read by the adult in earlier lessons.

Check: “sat” has the order s-a-t and “tap” has t-a-p. If blending is not secure, return to matching one familiar sound and letter with adult modelling. Correct pronunciation guidance sensitively and check it with a knowledgeable teacher; differences in accent are not automatically lack of understanding. A letter-choice answer alone does not prove independent reading. Next step: repeat this small set before extending it.

Mathematics Lesson 1: Count and show zero

Learner aim: count up to five drawn objects, indicating each once, and recognise zero as no objects in a particular group. Move towards ten only after the smaller groups are comfortable; ten is an optional extension here, not a verified national target.

Model: draw three large circles in one box and an empty box beside it. Count the circles slowly, one indication per circle, then say “There are three circles in this box.” Indicate the empty box and say “There are no circles in this box. We can write zero.” The empty box still exists; zero refers to the number of circles, not to everything on the page.

Together: make boxes with one, two, four and five circles, keeping spaces clear. Ask the learner to count a selected box. Model tracking from one side to the other if a circle is skipped or counted twice. Rearrange three circles into a different shape and count again. The arrangement changed, but the quantity did not.

Try: ask the learner to draw or indicate a group of four. Then offer a choice between a box with no circles and a box with two circles for “zero circles”. A learner can direct an adult's drawing. Do not equate difficulty drawing circles with difficulty understanding four.

Check: the group with four circles represents four; the empty-circle box represents zero. Look for one-to-one tracking and a final quantity statement or selection. If support is needed, reduce the group and repeat with a stable layout. Optional extension: count two groups of five together to reach ten, while keeping each item visible and avoiding memorised recitation as the sole check.

Mathematics Lesson 2: Compare visible groups

Learner aim: identify more, fewer and the same quantity through counting or pairing visible pictures.

Model: draw two circles in Group A and four circles in Group B. Arrange them in two columns so each A circle has a partner in B. Point out the two unpaired B circles. Say “B has more circles; A has fewer circles.” The drawing's overall size or the space it takes is not the number of circles.

Together: count both groups and compare the totals. Then draw three circles in each of two groups. Make one group spread out and the other close together. Count or pair them to establish that they have the same quantity. Avoid introducing inequality symbols before the learner understands the comparison.

Try: prepare Group C with five circles and Group D with three. Ask which has more, which has fewer, and how the learner checked. A count, pair-matching demonstration or clear pointing explanation is acceptable. Offer “more / fewer / same” choices if needed.

Check and answers: B has more than A; A has fewer than B. The two groups of three have the same quantity, despite spacing. C has more than D; D has fewer than C. If the learner chooses the more widely spaced group as larger, bring the pictures into pairs and count again. Next step: repeat comparisons within five before extending the range.

Mathematics Lesson 3: Join and take away

Learner aim: represent joining and taking away with visible drawings and explain the result within five.

Model joining: draw two circles in one group and one in another. Say “There are two here and one more here. When we join them, there are three.” Count the combined group. Introduce “2 + 1 = 3” as a way to record the same action, rather than expecting the symbols to explain themselves.

Together: draw three circles and add two more. Count all five and record “3 + 2 = 5”. Then draw four circles on a new page and cross out one clearly. Say “We started with four. One is taken away. Three remain.” Count the circles that are not crossed out and record “4 − 1 = 3”. Crossed-out circles remain visible as a record but are not part of the remaining group.

Try: represent two joined with two, and five with two taken away. The learner can draw, direct an adult's drawing, select a picture or explain with a familiar communication method. Keep the initial and changed groups visible. Do not require mental calculation before the learner understands the action.

Check and answers: 2 + 2 = 4 and 5 − 2 = 3. Ask what was joined or removed, not only for the final answer. If every original circle is counted in the subtraction example, model identifying the uncrossed group. If difficulty concerns the symbols, return to the story and drawing. Next step: use another joining or taking-away example within five; no timed drill is required.

English practice sheet

Adult directions: read the story and prompts aloud if independent reading is not yet suitable. The learner may answer orally, point, sign, dictate or use an established communication aid. Copy onto paper with generous spaces.

Story: “Bala puts a cup on the table. Zainab brings a book. They look at a picture together.” Task E1: who puts the cup on the table? Task E2: what does Zainab bring? Task E3: does the story tell us the colour of the cup? Task E4: put these words into a clear sentence: “book / The / on / is / table / the”. Task E5: use or dictate your own sentence about a familiar object. Task E6: with adult sound modelling if needed, blend s-a-t and t-a-p. Write or select the whole words only if that is an appropriate response route.

Answer guidance: E1 Bala; E2 a book; E3 no, the colour is not stated; E4 “The book is on the table.” E5 has several valid answers and must match the learner's intended meaning. E6 “sat” and “tap”; record whether adult modelling was used. Do not infer independent reading from a modelled response. Word-order and phonics tasks are not a test of reading the entire story independently.

Mathematics practice sheet

Adult directions: draw each group using large, clearly separated circles before the session. No small counters are needed. Read the prompts and allow the learner to indicate or direct drawing.

Task M1: draw a box with four circles and ask how many circles it contains. Task M2: show a box containing no circles and ask for the number of circles. Task M3: draw two circles in Group A and five in Group B; ask which group has more. Task M4: draw three circles in each of two groups with different spacing; ask whether they have the same number. Task M5: show two circles joined by two more; ask for the total. Task M6: show five circles with one crossed out; ask how many remain.

Answer guidance: M1 four; M2 zero; M3 B; M4 yes, each has three; M5 four, recorded as 2 + 2 = 4; M6 four, recorded as 5 − 1 = 4. Use the actual drawings to check the quantities before presenting the sheet. The adult should not change a group's size while keeping its old answer note. Different arrangements with the same quantity are acceptable.

Learning check and next teaching step

For English, check whether the learner can find a stated story detail, communicate one understandable sentence and participate in the small sound-set activity with their recorded level of support. For Mathematics, check one-to-one counting, a visible-group comparison, and a joining or taking-away representation. Keep these observations separate: a learner may understand numbers while needing additional English support.

Use “demonstrated with current support”, “developing with modelling” or “not observed yet” as planning notes, not grades or diagnoses. Record the task, response method, adult support and next lesson choice. One correct guessed answer is insufficient evidence of a skill; use another suitable example. Repeat or adapt a short activity before increasing difficulty. A pause request, communication barrier or fatigue is not a failure.

Keep observation notes private using a local learner code where needed. Do not request full names, photos, voice recordings, home addresses or personal mental-health disclosures in these worksheets or upload observations to the public review page. No certificate, pass threshold, leaderboard or graded progression is provided.
